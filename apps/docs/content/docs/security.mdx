Security in knext is not a milestone to defer — these controls run through every phase. The
non-negotiable rule: **no unauthenticated mutating endpoints, ever.** Several controls are enforced
automatically; the rest are documented guarantees knext holds to.

## No unauthenticated mutating endpoints

Any route that changes state requires auth. Two app routes mutate cache state, and both require a
**Bearer token**:

| Endpoint | Method | Auth |
|---|---|---|
| `/api/cache/invalidate` | `POST` | Bearer `CACHE_INVALIDATE_TOKEN`, fail-closed |
| `/api/cache/events` | `DELETE` | same Bearer token |

The token check is:

- **Fail-closed** — if `CACHE_INVALIDATE_TOKEN` is unset or empty, **nothing is authorized**. An
  unconfigured deployment rejects all invalidations rather than leaving the endpoint open.
- **Constant-time** — comparison uses `timingSafeEqual` so the token cannot be recovered via
  response timing (length is checked first, which does not leak the secret).

The token is provisioned from a Kubernetes Secret into the `CACHE_INVALIDATE_TOKEN` env var — never
hardcoded.

<Callout type="warn">
There is intentionally **no `GET /api/cache/invalidate`** handler. A mutating GET is
prefetchable/link-triggerable and would leak the Bearer token into URLs and logs. App Router
returns `405` for the unexported GET; `POST` is the only invalidation entrypoint.
</Callout>

The browser RUM beacon `POST /api/rum` is a deliberate, justified exception — a bounded metrics
aggregator, not a write primitive. See [Observability → RUM](/docs/observability#rum-web-vitals)
for the full rationale.

## Network isolation

Auth is one factor; **network isolation** is the second. knext reconciles a default-on Kubernetes
`NetworkPolicy` for your app automatically.

- **Object:** a `NetworkPolicy` named `<app>-allow-ingress`, tied to your app's lifecycle
  (garbage-collected on delete).
- **Targets:** the app's serving pods (selected by `serving.knative.dev/service: <app>`).
- **Allows ingress from:** the `knative-serving` and `kourier-system` namespaces (so
  scale-from-zero traffic keeps flowing) on the serving and metrics ports, plus same-namespace
  pods on the **metrics ports only**. Everything else — arbitrary cross-namespace and external
  pod-direct traffic — is denied.
- **Ports are restricted, and your app's own port is not among them.** The policy admits the
  Knative queue-proxy ports (`8012`/`8013`, plus `8112` for internal TLS), the queue-proxy
  metrics port (`9090`) and knext's app-metrics port (`9464`). Your app's container port is deliberately **excluded**: the
  queue-proxy reaches it over pod-local loopback, which no `NetworkPolicy` governs. The effect
  is that a pod sharing your namespace can no longer dial your app container directly and
  bypass the queue-proxy — and with it your concurrency limit.
- **Scraping from another namespace requires a label on that namespace.** If your Prometheus runs
  outside your app's namespace, label its namespace and it may scrape the app metrics port
  (`9464`) — and only that:

  ```bash
  kubectl label namespace monitoring knext.dev/metrics-scrape=true
  ```

  Without the label, cross-namespace scraping is denied — including for knext's own shipped
  `PodMonitor`. A labelled namespace never gains your app's serving ports.

<Callout type="warn">
**Know what this label is and is not.** It is a **cluster-level** grant, not per-app consent:

- The label lives on the **Namespace** object, so whoever can `update namespaces` decides — normally
  a cluster admin, but on a platform with self-service namespace creation a tenant can create and
  label its own.
- Once a namespace is labelled, **every pod in it** — not only Prometheus — can scrape `9464` on
  **every knext app in the cluster**. The policy matches the namespace, not a specific workload,
  because knext cannot know how your Prometheus pods are labelled.
- An individual app owner has no per-app opt-out short of `spec.security.networkPolicy: false`.

If you need tighter identity than "any pod in a trusted namespace", **adding your own
`NetworkPolicy` will not help**: Kubernetes policies are *additive*, so a second policy unions its
allow-rules with knext's rather than narrowing them. The only way to tighten this is to turn knext's
policy off (`spec.security.networkPolicy: false`) and write your own that expresses exactly the
access you want.

Labelling no namespace keeps the previous behaviour exactly.

Removing the label denies new scrapes, but a connection already established may survive until it is
closed — Kubernetes CNIs evaluate policy when a connection is opened, not on every packet.
</Callout>
- **Toggle:** `spec.security.networkPolicy` (`*bool`). `nil` (unset) or `true` reconciles the
  policy (**default-on**); `false` skips it and deletes any existing one on the next reconcile.

<Callout type="warn">
**If a pod in your namespace calls your app directly on its container port, that call will stop
working.** This is the point of the restriction, not a side effect — but if you rely on it, either
call the app through its service URL (routed via the gateway, which is allowed) or set
`spec.security.networkPolicy: false` to opt out entirely.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn">
**Honest scope: L3/L4, not L7.** A `NetworkPolicy` filters by source pod/namespace at the network
layer — it **cannot** target an HTTP path. It does not isolate `/api/cache/invalidate` per se; it
makes the **whole pod** unreachable for disallowed direct traffic (a leaked token is useless to an
attacker who cannot route to the pod). It is also only enforced if your cluster **CNI** supports
NetworkPolicy (Calico, Cilium, etc.) — on a non-enforcing CNI the policy is a no-op.
</Callout>

**knext tells you which case you are in** instead of leaving it silent. The operator reports a
`NetworkPolicyEnforced` condition on every `NextApp`: `True` when a NetworkPolicy-enforcing agent
(Calico, Cilium, kube-router, Weave Net, Antrea, Canal) is detected on the cluster, `False` when
flannel is the CNI — there the policy is **declarative only**: it is written but enforces nothing —
and `Unknown` when the CNI cannot be identified. Treat `Unknown` as unenforced until you verify it;
a detector that guessed "enforced" would be worse than none. `knext doctor` runs the same
read-only detection as a preflight check. Note that flannel is what OrbStack and stock OKE clusters
run — on those, treat network isolation as absent unless you install a policy-capable CNI.

## Read-only root filesystem

Defense in depth against in-container tampering: knext renders your app's container with a
**read-only root filesystem** by default.

- **Toggle:** `spec.security.readOnlyRootFilesystem` (`*bool`). `nil` (unset) or `true` renders
  `readOnlyRootFilesystem: true` on the container (**default-on**); `false` keeps a writable root,
  the pre-existing behavior.
- **No extra volume is mounted by default.** Writable scratch volumes cost pod-startup time on
  every scale-from-zero wake, so knext only provisions one when your app's image actually needs it.
  When `knext deploy` builds the image itself and knows it writes nothing to local disk, it sets
  `spec.security.writeFree: true` on your `NextApp`, and the operator then mounts **no** writable
  volume at all. That covers:
  - **vinext apps built as a disk-mode binary** (the default): image optimization loads its native
    library from the image's read-only `native/` directory.
  - **Standalone (Node/Bun) apps with object storage** (`spec.storage`) that use the knext cache
    handler (every `knext create` app does): the knext adapter turns on Next's
    `images.customCacheHandler` option, so optimized images are stored through the cache handler
    instead of `.next/cache/images` on disk. On the Bun runtime with Redis configured they are
    shared by every pod and kept across scale-to-zero. On the Node runtime, and without Redis,
    they are kept in each pod's memory, capped at
    32 MiB by default (set `KNEXT_IMAGE_CACHE_MEMORY_BYTES` to change it). To keep Next's disk
    cache instead, build with `KNEXT_IMAGE_CACHE_HANDLER=0` and set `writableCache: true`.
  - **Self-contained standalone executables** (`selfContained: true` on the Bun runtime), which
    carry no native libraries.
- **Shapes that still get a volume.** knext keeps a writable volume when it cannot vouch for the
  image:
  - A **self-contained vinext binary** (`selfContained: true` with the vinext target) gets a
    writable, executable `/tmp`, because it unpacks a native library there on the first
    image-optimization request.
  - A standalone app with object storage whose build still writes images to disk (a custom
    `cacheHandler`, or a Next.js version without `images.customCacheHandler`) gets Next's cache
    directory, so the image optimizer can write the variant before knext syncs it to your bucket.
  - A deploy with `--image` or `--skip-build` never sets `writeFree`, so these shapes keep the
    volumes they had before.
- **Standalone apps without object storage** never got a volume. The image optimizer re-computes
  each variant per request unless the app uses the knext cache handler, and an app with no
  `cacheHandler` configured sees its default ISR/fetch-cache writes rejected (see the callout
  below).
- **Opt in to the old, always-mounted behavior** with `spec.security.writableCache: true` — this
  mounts both the `/tmp` scratch volume and (standalone target only) Next's cache directory
  unconditionally, regardless of your build shape, storage configuration or `writeFree`. Use this if you want
  the image-optimizer's local cache to persist across requests on the standalone target with no
  object storage configured, or if your app has no `cacheHandler` and you want its default
  on-disk ISR fallback to actually persist rather than fail silently.
- **What this does not affect:** the baked, build-time code cache your runtime warms into the image
  stays fully readable; a read-only root only removes the (already rare) ability to add a *new*
  entry to it at request time, never the ability to read what shipped.

<Callout type="warn">
**Known gap: an app with no `cacheHandler` configured** also has Next flush revalidated page HTML
back onto a build-output path this cannot make writable without deleting your app's prebuilt pages
— that specific write is rejected under a read-only root regardless of `writableCache`. It degrades
gracefully (Next logs the failure and keeps serving from its in-memory cache; the page still
returns successfully — ISR just falls back to memory-only instead of persisting across
pods/restarts), but if you rely on Redis-backed ISR, configure a `cacheHandler` — `knext create`
and the reference app do this by default — to avoid it entirely.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn">
**If your own code writes to a path under the app's working directory at runtime** (outside the
paths above), including `/tmp` (`os.tmpdir()`), a read-only root filesystem will reject that write.
No app gets a writable `/tmp` by default except a self-contained vinext binary. Set
`spec.security.writableCache: true` to mount writable `/tmp` and `.next/cache` scratch space, or
set `spec.security.readOnlyRootFilesystem: false` to opt out entirely.
</Callout>

## Digest pinning

knext rejects mutable image tags everywhere.

- **`:latest` is rejected.** An app whose `image` is `:latest`, tag-only, or untagged is marked
  `Degraded` (reason `InvalidImage`) and never deployed — a ref is accepted only if it is
  digest-pinned (`@sha256:`).
- **knext's own images are digest-pinned too.** A build-time check fails if any internal deployment
  manifest references a non-pinned tag, so knext never ships a mutable image reference.

See [Operator → Security defaults](/docs/operator#security-defaults).

## Supply chain

Both the **app image** and the **operator image** go through the same supply-chain pipeline, with
a strict ordering: **build → SBOM → scan gate → push → sign**. Nothing reaches the registry before
it passes the scan, and signing happens on the digest that was actually pushed.

- **SBOM per image** — generated with **syft** (SPDX-JSON), published as an artifact and attested.
- **Vulnerability scan as a push gate** — **Trivy** scans the exact image (as a local OCI layout)
  for `HIGH,CRITICAL` findings **before** it is pushed. A failing scan means the image is never
  published — not "published but flagged."
- **Signing + attestation on the pushed digest** — **cosign** keyless signing (OIDC / Sigstore) of
  the digest that was pushed, plus an SBOM attestation (`cosign attest --type spdxjson`) bound to
  that digest.
- **SLSA provenance** — the build carries a BuildKit SLSA provenance attestation, and the pipeline
  asserts it survived the push before anything is signed.
- **Digest-pinned release bundle** — the operator's `install.yaml` release asset is regenerated
  with the **real pushed digest** of the operator image, so what you `kubectl apply` is exactly
  what was scanned and signed. See [Installing knext](/docs/install#4--install-the-operator).

## Runtime hardening

- **Distroless, non-root.** The operator image is `gcr.io/distroless/static:nonroot` running as
  UID `65532`; the app image runs as non-root UID `65532`.
- **No token automount.** knext sets `AutomountServiceAccountToken: false` on the app's
  ServiceAccount.
- **Graceful shutdown.** On `SIGTERM` the server **drains in-flight requests and runs `after()`
  callbacks** before exit, then closes the metrics server — no dropped requests on scale-down. On
  the standalone targets the supervisor forwards the signal to Next.js; on the vinext targets (the
  Bun executable and the Node server) the app's server entry does the drain itself. It waits for
  every `after()` callback, including work that an `after()` callback schedules while the drain
  is already running. The drain has a hard cap (`SHUTDOWN_GRACE_MS`) kept below the pod's
  `terminationGracePeriodSeconds`; on the vinext targets, work still running at the cap is cut off
  and the process exits with code 1. On the standalone targets the process still exits with code 0
  at the cap, but first logs a warning whose message starts with `[knext] shutdown` (where the
  server writes JSON logs, that text is the log line's `msg` field) and says what it cut off:
  the number of in-flight requests it dropped where the process can count them, or that the
  number is unknown where it cannot. If that warning shows up in your pod logs, raise
  `SHUTDOWN_GRACE_MS` or find the request or `after()` callback that never finishes.
  Apps created before this behaviour shipped carry an older `knext-bun-entry.mjs` that exits
  without waiting for `after()` work. Copy `knext-bun-entry.mjs` and `runtime-contract.mjs` from a
  freshly created app into yours to pick it up.

## Secrets

Secrets live in **Kubernetes Secrets / env only** — never in config files, source, container
images, or URLs. knext provisions them (via `spec.secrets` — `envFrom` / `envMap`) and the app
reads them from the environment.

## Operator permissions

The operator runs with a least-privilege `ClusterRole` (`config/rbac/role.yaml` in the install
bundle) and, in particular, **no permission on core Secrets** — no `get`, `list`, `watch`, or any
write verb, cluster-wide or in any namespace. Your `spec.secrets` entries are passed to the
kubelet as references (`envFrom.secretRef`, `valueFrom.secretKeyRef`), and the kubelet reads the
Secret when your pod starts. You can verify what an installed operator may do:

```sh
kubectl auth can-i list secrets --all-namespaces \
  --as=system:serviceaccount:kn-next-operator-system:kn-next-operator-controller-manager
# no
```

What the operator *does* still hold, so you can size the risk accurately: create/update/delete on
the Knative Services, ServiceAccounts, NetworkPolicies, image-prewarm DaemonSets, and Kafka
sources it manages, and read-only access to PersistentVolumeClaims and Knative Revisions.
Anyone allowed to create a `NextApp` can reference a Secret **in their own namespace** by name, so
restrict who may create `NextApp` resources with ordinary namespace RBAC.

<Callout type="info">
**Honest scope — not yet shipped.** Service-to-service **mTLS/authz** between the gateway and
backends, and the **cluster-local** gRPC backend services with no public ingress, are designed but
**not yet shipped**. Today's enforced boundary is endpoint auth plus the network policy above.
</Callout>
